# 1. Purpose

This framework establishes the governance structure for the Dunder Mifflin Enterprise Information Security and Risk Management Program.

It defines:

- Executive oversight
- Security decision authority
- Risk ownership
- Control accountability
- Policy authority
- The Information Security and Risk Committee
- Risk escalation
- Risk acceptance
- Security exception management
- Security reporting
- Assurance oversight

The objective is to ensure that information-security decisions support business objectives and that significant security risks are visible to the people authorized to make decisions about them.

---

# 2. Scope

This framework applies to security governance involving:

- Information
- Technology
- Applications
- Networks
- Endpoints
- Cloud services
- Business processes
- Facilities
- Personnel
- Physical security
- Third parties
- Business continuity
- Disaster recovery
- Secure development
- Security assurance
- Enterprise risk

The framework applies across Dunder Mifflin Headquarters and all branch locations.

---

# 3. Governance Objective

Security governance establishes:

- Who has authority
- Who is accountable
- How decisions are made
- How risk is escalated
- How exceptions are approved
- How management receives security information
- How control effectiveness is evaluated
- How unresolved security issues receive appropriate attention

Security governance must support informed business decisions rather than operate as an isolated Information Technology function.

---

# 4. Governance Principles

Dunder Mifflin security governance follows these principles:

1. Security exists to support business objectives.
2. Risk decisions belong to authorized management.
3. Security responsibilities must be clearly assigned.
4. Accountability must remain identifiable even when several departments participate.
5. Security controls must be proportionate to risk.
6. Business criticality must influence security priorities.
7. Proposed controls do not receive current risk-reduction credit until implemented and appropriately validated.
8. Material security deficiencies must remain visible until appropriately resolved or formally accepted.
9. Management reporting must provide information appropriate to the decision being made.
10. Human life and safety take priority over systems, facilities, equipment, inventory, revenue, and other organizational assets.

---

# 5. Governance Structure

Dunder Mifflin security governance operates through the following structure:

**CEO**

↓

**Information Security and Risk Committee**

↓

**CIO and Other Executive Risk Owners**

↓

**IT & Information Security Manager and Business Managers**

↓

**Control Owners and Operational Personnel**

The structure provides executive authority while allowing security activities to be performed by the personnel closest to the applicable systems and business processes.

---

# 6. Chief Executive Officer

The Chief Executive Officer (CEO) provides ultimate executive authority for the enterprise security program.

The CEO is responsible for:

- Approving the Enterprise Information Security and Risk Management Program
- Approving major enterprise security policies
- Establishing executive expectations for security and risk management
- Resolving significant cross-functional security issues
- Approving High risk acceptance after Information Security and Risk Committee review
- Approving Critical risk acceptance after Information Security and Risk Committee review
- Reviewing material security conditions requiring executive action
- Supporting appropriate security resources

The CEO does not perform routine security administration.

---

# 7. Chief Information Officer

The Chief Information Officer (CIO) provides executive leadership for information security and technology risk.

The CIO is responsible for:

- Leading the Enterprise Information Security and Risk Management Program
- Coordinating security governance
- Chairing or coordinating the Information Security and Risk Committee
- Overseeing cybersecurity and technology-security programs
- Coordinating enterprise security risk assessment
- Escalating significant security risks
- Overseeing security policy development
- Overseeing security assurance
- Coordinating security reporting
- Ensuring material findings receive appropriate management attention
- Coordinating security-program improvement

The CIO may delegate operational responsibilities while retaining executive accountability for assigned security functions.

---

# 8. Chief Operating Officer

The Chief Operating Officer (COO) provides executive oversight for operational and physical-security risks involving:

- Branch operations
- Warehousing
- Inventory
- Fulfillment
- Distribution
- Transportation
- Procurement operations
- Facility operations
- Supplier disruption
- Physical security
- Environmental security

The COO participates in enterprise security governance when security conditions affect these responsibilities.

---

# 9. Chief Financial Officer

The Chief Financial Officer (CFO) provides executive oversight for security risks involving:

- Accounting
- Accounts payable
- Payroll
- Financial transactions
- Vendor-payment processes
- Financial fraud controls
- Human Resources functions within the CFO organization
- Financial records

The CFO participates in security governance where financial, payroll, personnel, or fraud risks require management action.

---

# 10. Counsel and Compliance

Counsel and Compliance advises management regarding:

- Legal obligations
- Contractual obligations
- Regulatory matters
- Confidentiality
- Investigations
- Security incidents with potential legal significance
- Third-party contractual security requirements
- Records and evidence considerations

Counsel and Compliance supports security decision-making but does not replace the accountable business or risk owner.

---

# 11. IT & Information Security Manager

The IT & Information Security Manager coordinates day-to-day implementation of the information-security program.

Responsibilities include:

- Coordinating security operations
- Supporting risk assessment
- Maintaining security documentation
- Coordinating control implementation
- Coordinating vulnerability management
- Coordinating security monitoring
- Coordinating incident response
- Coordinating Identity and Access Management
- Coordinating recovery activities
- Supporting security assessments
- Tracking findings and corrective actions
- Preparing security information for management review

The IT & Information Security Manager reports material security conditions to the CIO.

---

# 12. Business Managers

Business managers are responsible for security within the business processes and resources under their authority.

Responsibilities include:

- Identifying business requirements
- Supporting asset ownership
- Authorizing appropriate access
- Participating in risk assessment
- Supporting treatment activities
- Maintaining business continuity capability
- Supporting investigations
- Correcting identified deficiencies
- Escalating conditions outside their authority

Information Technology ownership of a system does not remove business-management responsibility for the business process supported by that system.

---

# 13. Asset Owners

Asset Owners are accountable for determining:

- Business importance
- Authorized use
- Access requirements
- Information sensitivity
- Protection requirements
- Recovery requirements

Asset ownership is distinct from technical custody.

A cloud provider, system administrator, or other custodian may operate or maintain an asset without becoming its business owner.

---

# 14. Risk Owners

Each enterprise risk must have an identifiable accountable Risk Owner.

The Risk Owner is responsible for:

- Understanding the risk
- Reviewing the current risk rating
- Supporting treatment decisions
- Monitoring relevant changes
- Ensuring required actions receive appropriate management attention
- Escalating risk outside the owner's authority
- Participating in reassessment
- Supporting risk-acceptance decisions where applicable

Several departments may participate in treatment.

Shared participation does not eliminate the requirement for one accountable Risk Owner.

---

# 15. Control Owners

Each material security control should have an accountable Control Owner.

The Control Owner is responsible for:

- Understanding the control objective
- Supporting implementation
- Maintaining applicable procedures
- Ensuring required control activities occur
- Maintaining or producing appropriate evidence
- Supporting assessment
- Correcting identified deficiencies

A Control Owner may rely on other personnel to perform individual activities.

---

# 16. Information Security and Risk Committee

Dunder Mifflin establishes an Information Security and Risk Committee (ISRC) to provide coordinated management oversight of significant security and enterprise-risk matters.

The ISRC is a governance body.

It does not replace executive authority, Risk Owners, Control Owners, or operational management.

---

# 17. ISRC Membership

Standing participation should include:

- CIO
- COO
- CFO
- Counsel / Compliance
- IT & Information Security Manager

The CEO may participate directly when appropriate.

Other participants may be invited based on the matter under review, including:

- Business managers
- Asset Owners
- Risk Owners
- Control Owners
- Human Resources representatives
- Finance personnel
- Operations personnel
- Technical specialists
- External specialists

Participation should be based on business need.

---

# 18. ISRC Responsibilities

The ISRC is responsible for coordinated review of:

- Enterprise security risk
- Significant risk treatments
- High and Critical risk acceptance
- Material security incidents
- Significant findings
- Overdue corrective actions
- Major security exceptions
- Security metrics
- Security-program performance
- Business continuity and recovery readiness
- Significant third-party security issues
- Significant Infinity security issues
- Major security architecture changes
- Material changes in business or technology
- Security assurance results
- Security-program priorities

---

# 19. ISRC Meeting Frequency

The ISRC should meet quarterly.

Additional meetings may be called when required by:

- Significant incidents
- Critical findings
- Material business change
- Significant technology change
- High or Critical risk decisions
- Major third-party issues
- Significant recovery failures
- Other conditions requiring timely management action

---

# 20. ISRC Records

ISRC activity should be documented sufficiently to establish:

- Date
- Participants
- Matters reviewed
- Decisions
- Risk decisions
- Assigned actions
- Accountable owners
- Required follow-up

Records should be protected according to the sensitivity of the information discussed.

---

# 21. Decision Authority

Security decisions should be made at the lowest organizational level possessing sufficient authority to accept the associated business consequence.

Issues must be escalated when:

- Risk exceeds the person's authority.
- Required resources exceed available authority.
- Multiple departments are affected.
- Business priorities conflict.
- A significant security requirement cannot be satisfied.
- A material exception is requested.
- A treatment cannot achieve the expected result.
- A significant finding remains unresolved.

---

# 22. Risk Management Authority

Risk management operates under:

[[POL-005 Risk Management Policy]]

and:

[[RTP-001 Enterprise Risk Treatment Plan]]

Risk decisions must consider:

- Inherent risk
- Existing controls
- Current residual risk
- Proposed treatment
- Target residual risk
- Business impact
- Treatment cost
- Treatment feasibility
- Control effectiveness
- Applicable findings

---

# 23. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents exposure before considering existing controls.

Inherent risk remains relevant after controls are implemented because it helps management understand:

- The underlying importance of the scenario
- The degree of dependence on controls
- The consequence of control failure
- Assurance priority

A low current residual risk does not necessarily mean the underlying risk is unimportant.

---

# 24. Current Residual Risk

Current residual risk represents risk after considering controls that currently exist and receive appropriate credit.

Controls that are merely:

- Proposed
- Planned
- Approved
- Purchased
- Under implementation

do not automatically reduce current residual risk.

---

# 25. Target Residual Risk

Target residual risk is the predicted level of risk expected after proposed treatment is successfully implemented and validated.

Target residual risk is a planning estimate.

It does not become current residual risk merely because management approves the treatment.

---

# 26. Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance follows the authority established by:

[[POL-005 Risk Management Policy]]

| Risk Level | Acceptance Authority |
|---|---|
| Low | Risk Owner |
| Moderate | Risk Owner with appropriate executive management approval |
| High | CEO after ISRC review |
| Critical | CEO after ISRC review and only under exceptional circumstances |

High and Critical risk acceptance requires elevated oversight because of the potential business consequence.

---

# 27. High Risk Acceptance

Accepted High risk must be reassessed at least every 90 days while the acceptance remains applicable.

The review should determine whether:

- Conditions changed
- Treatment became feasible
- Compensating controls changed
- Business impact changed
- Continued acceptance remains appropriate

---

# 28. Critical Risk Acceptance

Accepted Critical risk must be reassessed at least every 30 days.

Critical risk acceptance should be exceptional.

The review should determine whether immediate additional action is required.

---

# 29. Risk Acceptance Documentation

Risk acceptance should document:

- Risk
- Current risk rating
- Reason for acceptance
- Existing controls
- Applicable compensating controls
- Business rationale
- Approving authority
- Acceptance date
- Review or expiration date
- Conditions requiring earlier reassessment

Acceptance does not mean the risk has been corrected.

---

# 30. Security Exceptions

A security exception is an authorized temporary or defined departure from an established security requirement.

Exceptions must not become an informal method for bypassing policy.

---

# 31. Exception Requirements

A material security exception should identify:

- Applicable requirement
- Business reason
- Scope
- Duration
- Associated risk
- Compensating controls where applicable
- Accountable owner
- Required approval
- Review or expiration date

---

# 32. Exception Approval

Exception approval should be proportionate to the associated risk.

Exceptions creating material enterprise risk must be escalated through the risk-management process.

An exception does not erase the underlying requirement.

---

# 33. Exception Expiration

Exceptions should have a defined review or expiration condition where practical.

Before continuation, management should determine whether:

- The exception remains necessary.
- The underlying condition has changed.
- A permanent solution is available.
- Risk remains acceptable.
- Compensating controls remain appropriate.

---

# 34. Policy Governance

Security policies establish management requirements.

Major enterprise security policies require CEO approval.

Supporting standards and procedures may be approved at the appropriate management level established by document ownership and subject matter.

---

# 35. Policy Hierarchy

The security documentation hierarchy is:

**Enterprise Security Program**

↓

**Policy**

↓

**Standard**

↓

**Procedure / Plan / Playbook**

↓

**Implementation**

↓

**Evidence**

Policies establish what is required.

Standards establish mandatory supporting requirements.

Procedures describe how required activities are performed.

---

# 36. Security Program Hierarchy

Security governance should maintain traceability across:

**Business Requirement → Asset / Process → Classification / BIA → Risk → Treatment → Policy / Standard → Control → Procedure → Implementation → Evidence → Test / Audit → Finding → Remediation → Retest → Risk Reassessment**

This relationship prevents security documents from becoming disconnected paperwork.

---

[[POL-007 Change & Configuration Management Policy]]

Change governance should consider:

- Business impact
- Security impact
- Testing
- Approval
- Rollback
- Recovery documentation
- Verification

# 38. Third-Party Governance

Third-party security is governed by:

[[POL-008 Third-Party Security Policy]]

Third-party oversight should consider:

- Business criticality
- Information access
- System access
- Operational dependency
- Incident risk
- Recovery dependency
- Contractual protection
- Exit capability

Third parties are classified as:

- Critical
- Important
- Routine

---

# 39. Confidentiality Governance

A Non-Disclosure Agreement or equivalent enforceable confidentiality obligation is required before a nonemployee or outside party receives sensitive access when appropriate to the access and information involved.

This requirement may apply to:

- Consultants
- Penetration testers
- Information Technology service providers
- Infinity contractors
- Service personnel with sensitive access
- Temporary specialists
- Other outside support personnel

An NDA does not replace technical, physical, personnel, or third-party security controls.

---

# 40. Incident Governance

Security incidents are governed by:

[[POL-009 Incident Response Policy]]

and:

[[PLN-001 Incident Response Plan]]

Material incidents must be escalated according to their severity, business impact, legal significance, and management requirements.

The ISRC should receive information about significant incidents and resulting corrective actions.

---

# 41. Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Governance

Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery are governed by:

[[POL-010 Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery Policy]]

Recovery requirements originate from:

[[RPT-002 Business Impact Analysis]]

Management must not select Recovery Time Objectives or Recovery Point Objectives independently of business requirements.

The sequence is:

**Business Requirement → Criticality → MTD → RTO / RPO → Recovery Strategy → Technical Design → Implementation → Testing**

---

# 42. Secure Development Governance

Application and secure-development governance is established by:

[[POL-012 Application & Secure Development Policy]]

and:

[[PRO-007 Secure Software Development Lifecycle Program]]

Infinity receives particular governance attention because it is:

- Internally owned
- Customer-facing
- Business-significant
- Supported by a third-party contractor

# 43. Infinity Ownership

Dunder Mifflin retains ownership of applicable Infinity:

- Source code
- Repository
- Intellectual property
- Application data
- Documentation
- Work product
- Backups

Contractor support must not create unacceptable dependency on the contractor.

---

# 44. Security Assurance Governance

Security assurance is governed by:

[[PRO-008 Security Assurance & Continuous Improvement Program]]

Assurance evaluates whether controls actually achieve their intended objectives.

The central assurance question is:

> **How do we know the control actually works?**

---

# 45. Assurance Independence

Assessment independence should be proportionate to:

- Risk
- Control importance
- Required expertise
- Potential conflict of interest
- Consequence of an incorrect conclusion

Independence does not require every assessment to be performed by an external organization.

---

# 46. Assurance Methods

Authorized assurance methods may include:

- Inspection
- Observation
- Interviews
- Sampling
- Independent Performance
- Technical Testing
- Exercises
- Reconciliation

The method should fit the assessment objective.

---

# 47. Annual Assurance Planning

Security assurance activities are coordinated through:

[[PLN-005 Annual Assurance Plan]]

Assessment priority should consider:

- Inherent risk
- Current residual risk
- Business criticality
- Critical controls
- Previous findings
- Material change
- Incident history
- Control maturity

---

# 48. Assessment Work Planning

Material assessments should use an appropriate work plan.

See:

[[TMP-001 Assessment Work Plan Template]]

The work plan should define:

- Objective
- Scope
- Requirement
- Control
- Evidence
- Assessment method
- Sample where applicable
- Expected result
- Reporting

---

# 49. Findings Governance

A finding is:

> **A documented conclusion supported by evidence concerning whether a requirement, control, process, or expected outcome is satisfied.**

Material deficiencies are tracked through:

[[REG-004 Finding & Corrective Action Register]]

---

# 50. Corrective Action

Material deficiencies should result in appropriate corrective action.

Corrective action should identify:

- Deficiency
- Accountable owner
- Required action
- Due date
- Expected evidence
- Validation method

Reported completion does not automatically establish successful correction.

---

# 51. Retesting

Where appropriate, corrective action must be retested.

The lifecycle is:

**Finding → Corrective Action → Validation → Retest → Closure**

A failed retest keeps the underlying issue visible and requires additional action.

---

# 52. Control Effectiveness

Control effectiveness is evaluated as:

- Effective
- Partially Effective
- Ineffective
- Not Implemented

Implementation status and effectiveness are separate concepts.

A control may be implemented and still be ineffective.

---

# 53. Control Inventory

Enterprise controls are maintained in:

[[REG-003 Enterprise Security Control Inventory]]

The control inventory supports traceability among:

- Requirements
- Controls
- Owners
- Risks
- Implementation status
- Evidence
- Assessment
- Effectiveness

---

# 54. Security Metrics

Approved security metrics are maintained in:

[[REG-005 Security Metrics Catalog]]

Metrics may include:

- Key Performance Indicators
- Key Risk Indicators

Metrics should support decisions rather than exist only to create reporting volume.

# 59. Reporting Chain

Security information should move through the organization using the following model:

**Technical Evidence → Operational Exception → Management Impact → Enterprise Risk → Executive Decision**

This prevents executive reporting from becoming overloaded with technical detail while preserving traceability to supporting evidence.

---

# 60. Escalation

Security matters should be escalated when:

- A risk exceeds the owner's authority.
- A High or Critical risk requires acceptance.
- A material control fails.
- A significant finding remains unresolved.
- A corrective action becomes materially overdue.
- A significant security incident occurs.
- A recovery test materially fails.
- A major third-party issue occurs.
- A security requirement cannot reasonably be satisfied.
- A material business or technology change alters risk.
- Required resources cannot be obtained at the current management level.

---

# 61. Security Decision Records

Material security decisions should be documented sufficiently to establish:

- Decision
- Decision maker
- Relevant risk
- Supporting information
- Required action
- Accountable owner
- Date
- Required follow-up

The amount of documentation should be proportionate to the significance of the decision.

---

# 62. Evidence Integrity

Security governance relies on trustworthy information.

Evidence must not be:

- Fabricated
- Backdated
- Improperly altered
- Manufactured to create the appearance that an activity occurred

Missing evidence must be reported as missing evidence.

---

# 63. Historical Integrity

Historical records should preserve:

- Original risk ratings
- Treatment decisions
- Failed tests
- Findings
- Corrective actions
- Retest results
- Risk reassessments

Improvement does not justify rewriting earlier conditions as though the weakness never existed.

---

# 64. Current and Proposed Controls

Governance reporting must distinguish between:

- Existing controls
- Proposed controls
- Controls being implemented
- Implemented controls awaiting validation
- Validated controls

This distinction protects the integrity of the risk model.

---

# 65. No Premature Risk Credit

Planned or proposed controls do not receive current residual-risk credit merely because management intends to implement them.

The sequence is:

**Treatment Planned**

↓

**Treatment Approved**

↓

**Control Implemented**

↓

**Evidence Collected**

↓

**Control Validated**

↓

**Effectiveness Determined**

↓

**Risk Reassessed**

---

# 66. Failed Validation

If validation shows that a control does not achieve its intended objective:

1. Record the result.
2. Determine whether a finding is required.
3. Assign corrective action.
4. Retain the appropriate effectiveness rating.
5. Retest after correction.
6. Reassess risk when supported by evidence.

Validation results must not be changed merely to support a desired risk rating.

---

# 67. Continuous Monitoring

Continuous monitoring means maintaining risk-appropriate awareness of:

- Security conditions
- Control performance
- Significant changes
- Findings
- Incidents
- Vulnerabilities
- Recovery capability
- Third-party conditions

It does not mean every system or control requires continuous human monitoring 24 hours a day.

---

# 68. Continuous Improvement

Security governance supports continuous improvement through:

**Risk → Treatment → Control → Evidence → Assessment → Finding → Corrective Action → Retest → Risk Reassessment**

Information from one cycle should improve the next.

---

# 69. Governance Review

This framework should be reviewed:

- Annually
- Following material organizational change
- Following material business change
- Following significant changes in enterprise risk
- Following major security-program change
- When governance responsibilities materially change

---

# 70. Governance Effectiveness

Governance effectiveness should be evaluated through evidence such as:

- ISRC records
- Risk decisions
- Treatment progress
- Risk-acceptance records
- Exception records
- Findings
- Corrective-action status
- Security metrics
- Reporting
- Assurance results

The existence of governance documents alone does not establish effective governance.
