# 1. Purpose

This policy establishes Dunder Mifflin's requirements for identifying, reporting, evaluating, containing, investigating, recovering from, and learning from information-security incidents.

Incident response must protect, in priority order:

1. Human life and safety
2. Business operations
3. Information
4. Systems
5. Facilities
6. Equipment
7. Inventory
8. Revenue and other company assets

Incident response must support timely action while preserving sufficient evidence and documentation for investigation, recovery, management decisions, legal requirements, and corrective action.

---

# 2. Scope

This policy applies to suspected or confirmed security incidents involving:

- Information
- Applications
- Servers
- Endpoints
- Networks
- Cloud services
- Authentication
- Email
- Infinity
- Third parties
- Contractors
- Physical security
- Facilities
- Mobile devices
- Backup systems
- Business operations
- Fraud-related security events
- Other Dunder Mifflin assets

The policy applies to employees, contractors, consultants, and other personnel with relevant responsibilities.

---

# 3. Objectives

Incident response must:

1. Provide a clear reporting path.
2. Evaluate suspicious activity promptly.
3. Classify incidents consistently.
4. Establish response authority.
5. Escalate significant incidents.
6. Contain damage.
7. Preserve evidence where required.
8. Support investigation.
9. Eradicate identified threats.
10. Restore affected services safely.
11. Coordinate third parties.
12. Document significant decisions.
13. Identify corrective actions.
14. Support risk reassessment.
15. Improve future response capability.

---

# 4. Security Event

A security event is an observable occurrence that may have security significance.

Examples include:

- Antivirus alert
- Failed authentication
- Suspicious email
- Unexpected system behavior
- Lost device
- Unusual network activity
- Physical-access concern
- Reported fraud attempt

A security event is not automatically a security incident.

---

# 5. Security Incident

A security incident is a security event or series of events that results in, or presents a meaningful likelihood of:

- Unauthorized access
- Unauthorized disclosure
- Unauthorized modification
- Destruction
- Service disruption
- Malware infection
- Credential compromise
- Fraud
- Security-control failure
- Other material harm to Dunder Mifflin

Incident determination must consider available evidence and business context.

---

# 6. Suspected Incidents

Personnel must report suspected security incidents even when they are uncertain whether an actual compromise occurred.

Personnel are not expected to perform their own technical investigation before reporting.

Early reporting is preferred to delayed reporting caused by uncertainty.

---

# 7. Incident Reporting

Personnel must promptly report suspected incidents through approved reporting channels.

Reports may be made to:

- Information Technology
- Information Security
- Appropriate manager
- Help desk
- Other designated incident contact

Urgent safety concerns must be reported through appropriate emergency channels before security investigation.

---

# 8. Reportable Events

Examples of events requiring reporting include:

- Suspected phishing
- Credential compromise
- Malware
- Ransomware
- Unauthorized access
- Sensitive information exposure
- Lost or stolen equipment
- Suspicious privileged activity
- Unauthorized configuration changes
- Significant third-party security incidents
- Physical intrusion
- Suspected security-related fraud
- Significant security-control failure
- Suspicious Infinity activity
- Loss of important security records
- Other activity reasonably suspected to affect security

---

# 9. Immediate Personnel Actions

Personnel who suspect an incident must:

- Report it promptly.
- Follow instructions from authorized responders.
- Avoid unnecessary alteration of potentially relevant evidence.
- Avoid discussing sensitive incident details with unauthorized personnel.
- Preserve relevant messages or records where practical.

Personnel must not conduct unauthorized security testing or investigation.

---

# 10. Safety

Human safety takes priority over:

- Evidence preservation
- System availability
- Equipment
- Information
- Facilities
- Inventory

Incident responders must not delay necessary safety actions solely to preserve technical evidence.

---

# 11. Incident Response Team

Dunder Mifflin maintains a virtual Incident Response Team (IRT).

The IRT is assembled according to the nature and severity of the incident rather than maintained as a dedicated full-time team.

---

# 12. Core Incident Response Roles

The IRT may include:

- CIO
- IT & Information Security Manager
- Information Security Analyst
- System Administrator
- Network/Security Administrator
- Information Technology Support
- Legal and Compliance
- Human Resources
- Finance
- Operations
- Communications or management representatives
- Relevant business owner
- External specialists

Participation depends on incident requirements.

---

# 13. Incident Response Leadership

The IT & Information Security Manager normally coordinates technical incident response.

The CIO provides executive security oversight and escalation.

Incident leadership may change when:

- Safety is involved.
- Legal issues predominate.
- Personnel investigation is required.
- Financial fraud is involved.
- Business continuity is activated.
- Executive authority is required.

Clear responsibility must be established for significant incidents.

---

# 14. Incident Response Authority

Authorized incident responders may take reasonable actions necessary to protect Dunder Mifflin.

Actions may include:

- Isolating systems
- Disabling accounts
- Blocking network access
- Removing malicious messages
- Preserving evidence
- Restricting third-party access
- Initiating recovery
- Requesting external assistance

Actions must remain proportionate to the incident.

---

# 15. Emergency Authority

When delay would materially increase harm, authorized responders may take necessary containment action before normal management approval can be obtained.

Emergency action must:

- Be proportionate.
- Be documented.
- Be reported to appropriate management as soon as practical.
- Receive subsequent review.

Emergency authority does not permit unrelated activity outside incident scope.

---

# 16. Incident Classification

Incidents must be classified according to:

- Business impact
- Information sensitivity
- Scope
- Operational disruption
- Privilege involved
- Number of systems affected
- Third-party involvement
- Recovery difficulty
- Legal or contractual implications
- Potential for continued harm

Detailed classification requirements are established in [[STD-008 Incident Classification & Escalation Standard]].

---

# 17. Incident Severity

Incident severity must guide:

- Escalation
- Response priority
- Management notification
- External assistance
- Documentation
- Recovery
- Post-incident review

Severity may change as new information becomes available.

Initial classification must not prevent later escalation or reduction.

---

# 18. Incident Escalation

Incidents must be escalated when:

- Impact increases.
- Sensitive information is involved.
- Privileged access is compromised.
- Multiple systems are affected.
- Critical business operations are affected.
- A third party is materially involved.
- Significant fraud is suspected.
- Legal or contractual obligations may apply.
- Recovery becomes difficult.
- Management authority is required.

Escalation must occur soon enough to support meaningful action.

---

# 19. Executive Notification

The CIO must ensure executive management is notified of significant incidents according to severity and business impact.

Executive notification should provide:

- What is known
- What remains uncertain
- Business impact
- Actions underway
- Significant decisions required
- Expected next update

Unverified speculation must be clearly distinguished from confirmed information.

---

# 20. Legal and Compliance Coordination

Legal and Compliance must be involved when an incident may involve:

- Legal obligations
- Contractual notification
- Litigation
- Law enforcement
- Sensitive personnel matters
- Significant information exposure
- Regulatory concerns
- Evidence requiring special handling

Technical personnel must not independently make legal determinations outside their responsibility.

---

# 21. Human Resources Coordination

Human Resources must be involved when an incident materially involves:

- Employee conduct
- Personnel records
- Employment action
- Insider activity
- Employee investigation
- Termination or suspension

Security personnel must coordinate personnel actions with Human Resources and management.

---

# 22. Finance Coordination

Finance must be involved when an incident involves or may involve:

- Business Email Compromise
- Fraudulent payments
- Vendor fraud
- Payroll fraud
- Financial-account changes
- Financial loss
- Accounting records

Financial containment may require coordination with financial institutions or payment providers.

---

# 23. Operations Coordination

Operations must be involved when an incident materially affects:

- Warehouses
- Inventory
- Shipping
- Receiving
- Transportation
- Branch operations
- Supplier operations
- Manual continuity processes

Technical response must consider operational consequences.

---

# 24. Incident Response Lifecycle

Dunder Mifflin uses the following general incident-response lifecycle:

**Preparation → Identification & Analysis → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Post-Incident Review**

Activities may overlap depending on incident conditions.

Responders must use judgment rather than delay necessary action solely to preserve a rigid sequence.

---

# 25. Preparation

Incident preparation must include, as appropriate:

- Response roles
- Contact information
- Escalation paths
- Response procedures
- Playbooks
- Evidence-handling capability
- Recovery information
- External support contacts
- Training
- Exercises

Preparation requirements are documented in [[PLN-001 Incident Response Plan]].

---

# 26. Identification and Analysis

Responders must determine, as information becomes available:

- What occurred
- When it occurred
- How it was detected
- Systems affected
- Accounts affected
- Information affected
- Business processes affected
- Likely scope
- Potential cause
- Whether activity is continuing

Analysis must distinguish known facts from assumptions.

---

# 27. Containment

Containment must limit continued harm while considering business impact and evidence needs.

Containment may include:

- Network isolation
- Account disablement
- Credential reset
- Blocking malicious traffic
- Email containment
- Access restriction
- Service isolation
- Third-party access suspension
- Physical-access restriction

Containment decisions must remain proportionate to risk.

---

# 28. Short-Term and Longer-Term Containment

Incident response may require:

- Immediate containment to stop active harm
- Longer-term containment allowing controlled operation while remediation occurs

Responders must consider whether temporary measures create unacceptable operational or security risk.

---

# 29. Eradication

Eradication must address the identified cause of the incident where practical.

Actions may include:

- Removing malware
- Removing unauthorized accounts
- Correcting vulnerabilities
- Correcting configurations
- Replacing compromised credentials
- Removing unauthorized persistence
- Applying necessary patches

Eradication must not be declared complete solely because visible symptoms disappear.

---

# 30. Recovery

Recovery must restore affected systems and business processes safely.

Recovery activities may include:

- Restoring systems
- Restoring data
- Rebuilding systems
- Reconnecting services
- Re-enabling accounts
- Validating security controls
- Monitoring for recurrence
- Verifying business operation

Recovery priorities must align with [[RPT-002 Business Impact Analysis]].

---

# 31. Recovery Validation

Before returning materially affected systems to normal operation, responders must verify, as appropriate:

- Required functionality
- Security controls
- Authentication
- Data integrity
- Required connectivity
- Removal of identified malicious activity
- Monitoring capability

Business owners should confirm restoration of significant business functions where appropriate.

---

# 32. Backup Use During Incidents

Backup restoration must consider whether backup data may contain:

- Malware
- Corrupted data
- Unauthorized changes
- Compromised configuration

The existence of a backup does not automatically establish that the backup is safe to restore.

Backup and recovery requirements are established in [[PROC-012 Backup & Recovery Procedure]].

---

# 33. Business Continuity Activation

An incident may require activation of business-continuity procedures when normal systems or facilities cannot support required business operations.

Continuity decisions must follow:

- [[POL-010 Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery Policy]]
- [[PLN-002 Business Continuity Plan]]
- [[PLN-003 Disaster Recovery Plan]]

Incident response and continuity activities must be coordinated.

---

# 34. Manual Processing

When an incident requires manual business processing, affected functions must follow [[PROC-013 Manual Processing Procedure]].

Manual records must be:

- Protected
- Attributed
- Reviewed
- Reconciled
- Entered into restored systems where required
- Verified

Manual continuity must not create uncontrolled information or transaction risk.

---

# 35. Evidence Preservation

Potentially relevant evidence must be preserved when required for:

- Investigation
- Root-cause analysis
- Legal review
- Personnel action
- Insurance
- Law enforcement
- Corrective action

Evidence preservation must remain proportionate to incident significance.

---

# 36. Evidence Handling

Evidence must be handled in a manner that supports:

- Integrity
- Traceability
- Accountability
- Reliable analysis

Detailed requirements are established in [[PROC-011 Evidence Handling Procedure]].

---

# 37. Evidence Documentation

Evidence records should identify, as applicable:

- Item
- Source
- Date and time collected
- Collector
- Storage location
- Transfers
- Relevant handling actions

Documentation depth must reflect evidence significance.

---

# 38. Digital Evidence Integrity

Cryptographic hashes should be used where practical to support integrity verification of collected digital evidence.

SHA-256 should be used where supported.

Hashing supports evidence integrity but does not independently prove:

- Who created the evidence
- Whether the original source was trustworthy
- Whether the evidence is relevant
- Whether the evidence is complete

---

# 39. Evidence Access

Access to incident evidence must be limited to personnel with legitimate need.

Evidence containing:

- Authentication information
- Sensitive employee information
- Customer information
- Security vulnerabilities
- Legal information

must receive appropriate protection under [[POL-004 Information Classification & Handling Policy]].

---

# 40. Chain of Custody

Formal chain-of-custody documentation must be used when the nature of the incident or expected use of evidence requires it.

Chain-of-custody records should document:

- Collection
- Possession
- Transfer
- Storage
- Access
- Disposition

Not every routine security event requires formal evidentiary chain of custody.

---

# 41. Incident Timeline

Significant incidents must maintain a timeline.

The timeline should document important events including:

- Detection
- Reporting
- Analysis
- Escalation
- Containment
- Management decisions
- Eradication
- Recovery
- External coordination

The timeline should distinguish confirmed times from estimates where necessary.

---

# 42. Decision Log

Significant incidents should document material response decisions.

The record should identify:

- Decision
- Time
- Decision maker
- Available information
- Reason for decision

Documentation must not prevent urgent action.

---

# 43. External Incident Response Support

Dunder Mifflin may use a retained Digital Forensics and Incident Response (DFIR) provider for incidents requiring expertise or capacity beyond internal resources.

The provider may assist with:

- Digital forensics
- Malware analysis
- Investigation
- Containment advice
- Evidence preservation
- Recovery support

Dunder Mifflin retains incident-management authority.

---

# 44. DFIR Provider Activation

The DFIR provider should be considered when an incident involves:

- Significant compromise
- Ransomware
- Widespread malware
- Privileged compromise
- Significant data exposure
- Complex forensic requirements
- Uncertain scope
- Potential legal significance
- Internal resource limitations

Activation must be authorized according to the Incident Response Plan.

---

# 45. Third-Party Incidents

Security incidents originating with or affecting a third party must be evaluated as Dunder Mifflin incidents when company systems, information, or material services may be affected.

Third-party requirements are established in [[POL-008 Third-Party Security Policy]].

---

# 46. Third-Party Notification

Critical and Important third parties must notify Dunder Mifflin of relevant security incidents within 24 hours of discovery.

Dunder Mifflin must not wait for a third party to complete its investigation before beginning its own assessment and response.

---

# 47. Third-Party Cooperation

Affected third parties may be required to provide:

- Incident details
- Logs
- Timeline information
- Technical evidence
- Containment status
- Recovery information
- Corrective-action information

Third-party information must be independently evaluated where appropriate.

---

# 48. Communications

Incident communications must be coordinated.

Sensitive incident information must be limited to personnel with legitimate need.

Communications must:

- Distinguish confirmed information from assumptions.
- Avoid unnecessary speculation.
- Protect sensitive information.
- Support consistent management decisions.

---

# 49. External Communications

Public statements concerning significant incidents must be authorized by appropriate management.

Employees and technical responders must not make unauthorized public statements concerning:

- Incident scope
- Affected information
- Attribution
- Financial impact
- Legal responsibility
- Recovery status

Legal and management review must occur where appropriate.

---

# 50. Customer and Third-Party Communications

Notification to customers, suppliers, business partners, or other outside parties must be coordinated with appropriate:

- Management
- Legal and Compliance
- Business owners

Notification decisions must consider:

- Known facts
- Contractual obligations
- Legal requirements
- Business impact

---

# 51. Law Enforcement

Contact with law enforcement must be coordinated with appropriate management and Legal and Compliance unless immediate safety circumstances require emergency contact.

The decision should consider:

- Nature of incident
- Potential criminal activity
- Evidence
- Business impact
- Legal considerations

---

# 52. Attribution

Dunder Mifflin must avoid unsupported attribution of an incident to a specific person, organization, or external actor.

Attribution conclusions must be based on sufficient evidence.

Technical indicators alone may not establish identity.

---

# 53. Personnel Investigations

Suspected employee misconduct must be coordinated with:

- Human Resources
- Legal and Compliance
- Appropriate management

Security personnel must preserve relevant evidence while remaining within authorized investigative scope.

---

# 54. Privacy

Incident investigation must remain proportionate to legitimate security and business needs.

Incident response must not become indiscriminate surveillance.

Access to personnel information must be limited to authorized purposes.

---

# 55. Credential Compromise

Suspected credential compromise requires prompt evaluation.

Response may include:

- Account disablement
- Password reset
- Session termination where supported
- Review of authentication activity
- Review of affected access
- Investigation of related phishing
- Review of privileged access
- Monitoring for recurrence

Detailed response is established in [[PBK-001 Phishing & Credential Compromise Playbook]].

---

# 56. Malware and Ransomware

Suspected malware or ransomware requires prompt containment and analysis.

Response may include:

- Isolation
- Malware identification
- Scope determination
- Credential review
- Backup protection
- Eradication
- Rebuild
- Recovery
- Monitoring

Detailed response is established in [[PBK-002 Malware & Ransomware Playbook]].

---

# 57. Unauthorized Access

Suspected unauthorized access requires evaluation of:

- Account
- Privilege
- Systems accessed
- Information accessed
- Authentication activity
- Persistence
- Related configuration changes
- Scope

Detailed response is established in [[PBK-003 Unauthorized Access Playbook]].

---

# 58. Data Exposure

Suspected sensitive information exposure requires evaluation of:

- Information involved
- Classification
- Quantity
- Recipients
- Duration
- Access
- Business impact
- Required containment
- Notification considerations

Detailed response is established in [[PBK-004 Data Exposure Playbook]].

---

# 59. Third-Party Incident Playbook

Incidents involving third-party services or access must follow [[PBK-005 Third-Party Incident Playbook]] where applicable.

Response must consider:

- Provider containment
- Dunder Mifflin containment
- Access suspension
- Service dependency
- Evidence availability
- Recovery
- Contract requirements
- Corrective action

---

# 60. Fraud-Related Incidents

Suspected vendor, sales, or payroll fraud involving information systems or security controls must be coordinated with appropriate business management.

Security response may support:

- Account review
- Email analysis
- Access review
- Evidence preservation
- Transaction investigation

Financial control and personnel decisions remain with the appropriate business functions.

---

# 61. Physical Security Incidents

Physical security incidents may include:

- Unauthorized entry
- Lost access credential
- Restricted-area access
- Equipment theft
- Inventory theft
- Suspicious visitor activity

Response must coordinate with Operations, management, Human Resources, or law enforcement where appropriate.

Physical-security requirements are established in [[POL-011 Physical & Environmental Security Policy]].

---

# 62. Incident Documentation

Significant incidents must have an incident record.

The record should identify:

- Incident identifier
- Description
- Detection source
- Date and time
- Severity
- Affected assets
- Affected business processes
- Information involved
- Response actions
- Decisions
- Evidence
- Recovery
- Findings
- Corrective actions
- Closure decision

Documentation depth must remain proportionate to incident significance.

---

# 63. Incident Status

Incident status must be clearly tracked.

Statuses may include:

- Reported
- Under Analysis
- Contained
- Eradication in Progress
- Recovery in Progress
- Monitoring
- Closed

Status must reflect actual response condition.

---

# 64. Incident Closure

A significant incident may be closed when appropriate determination has been made that:

- Active threat has been addressed.
- Required containment is complete.
- Required recovery is complete.
- Significant evidence is preserved.
- Required notifications are addressed.
- Material findings are documented.
- Corrective actions are assigned or completed as appropriate.

Closure does not require every long-term corrective action to be completed.

Outstanding corrective actions must remain tracked after incident closure.

---

# 65. Post-Incident Review

Significant incidents must receive post-incident review.

The review should determine:

- What occurred
- Why it occurred
- How it was detected
- What controls worked
- What controls failed
- Whether escalation was appropriate
- Whether response was effective
- Whether recovery met business needs
- What corrective actions are required
- Whether risk must be reassessed

---

# 66. Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis must focus on meaningful contributing causes rather than simply assigning blame.

Possible causes may include:

- Vulnerability
- Configuration error
- Process failure
- Access-control weakness
- Human error
- Inadequate training
- Third-party failure
- Missing control
- Inadequate monitoring
- Design weakness

Corrective action should address actual causes where practical.

---

# 67. Lessons Learned

Lessons from significant incidents must be considered for:

- Policy changes
- Procedure changes
- Technical controls
- Training
- Monitoring
- Architecture
- Third-party requirements
- Business continuity
- Recovery
- Risk treatment

Lessons learned must result in action when material improvement is required.

---

# 68. Findings

Material control deficiencies identified during incident response must be documented as findings.

Examples include:

- Missing required control
- Ineffective control
- Excessive access
- Inadequate logging
- Failed backup
- Inadequate recovery
- Uncontrolled configuration
- Missing third-party requirement
- Repeated process failure

Material corrective actions must be tracked in [[REG-004 Finding & Corrective Action Register]].

---

# 69. Corrective Action

Corrective action must identify, as appropriate:

- Finding
- Affected control
- Affected risk
- Responsible party
- Required remediation
- Due date
- Expected evidence
- Validation requirement

Corrective action must address the underlying issue rather than merely close the incident record.

---

# 70. Risk Reassessment

Significant incidents must be evaluated to determine whether affected risks require reassessment.

Reassessment may be required when:

- Likelihood assumptions changed.
- Impact assumptions changed.
- Existing controls failed.
- New vulnerabilities were identified.
- New dependencies were discovered.
- Threat conditions changed.

Risk reassessment must follow [[POL-005 Risk Management Policy]].

---

# 71. Incident Metrics

Incident-response metrics may include:

- Incidents by severity
- Incidents by type
- Detection source
- Time to identification
- Time to containment
- Time to recovery
- Repeat incidents
- Incidents involving third parties
- Incidents involving compromised credentials
- Incidents causing continuity activation
- Open corrective actions

Metric definitions are maintained in [[REG-005 Security Metrics Catalog]].

---

# 72. Metric Interpretation

Incident metrics must be interpreted in context.

For example:

- More reported incidents may reflect better detection.
- Faster closure does not necessarily mean better investigation.
- Low incident counts do not independently prove strong security.
- A severe incident may matter more than many minor events.

Metrics must support decisions rather than encourage misleading performance behavior.

---

# 73. Incident Trend Analysis

Incident trends must be reviewed for:

- Recurring causes
- Repeated control failures
- Common attack methods
- Repeated user behavior
- Third-party patterns
- Business-process weaknesses
- Treatment effectiveness

Recurring incidents may require broader corrective action.

---

# 74. Exercises

Incident-response capability must be exercised periodically.

Exercises may include:

- Tabletop exercises
- Technical exercises
- Communication exercises
- Playbook walkthroughs
- Recovery coordination

Exercise complexity must reflect risk and available resources.

---

# 75. Exercise Evidence

Exercise records should identify:

- Scenario
- Participants
- Date
- Objectives
- Actions
- Observations
- Findings
- Corrective actions

Exercises must not be recorded as successful merely because they were conducted.

---

# 76. Training

Incident responders must receive training appropriate to assigned responsibilities.

Training may address:

- Incident classification
- Escalation
- Containment
- Evidence handling
- Communication
- Recovery
- Playbook use
- Documentation

General personnel reporting requirements are established in [[POL-006 Security Awareness & Training Policy]].

---

# 77. Assessment

Incident-response controls must be periodically assessed.

Assessment may include:

- Inspection of incident records
- Sampling
- Interviews
- Observation
- Exercise review
- Technical Testing
- Independent Performance
- Review of corrective actions

Assessment must evaluate actual response capability rather than documentation alone.

---

# 78. Evidence

Incident-response evidence may include:

- Incident records
- Logs
- Emails
- System images
- Malware samples
- Authentication records
- Network records
- Timeline records
- Decision logs
- Third-party notifications
- Exercise records
- Recovery records
- Findings
- Corrective actions

Evidence must be protected according to sensitivity.

---

# 79. Evidence Integrity

Personnel must not:

- Manufacture incident records.
- Alter evidence to conceal control failure.
- Backdate incident actions.
- Record containment that did not occur.
- Conceal significant incident facts.
- Represent planned corrective action as completed.

Uncertainty must be documented as uncertainty rather than converted into unsupported fact.

---

This policy provides significant treatment support for risks including:

- R001 Phishing / Credential Compromise
- R002 Business Email Compromise
- R003 Ransomware
- R004 Malware
- R005 Password / Authentication Weakness
- R006 Infinity Application Compromise
- R007 Internet Infrastructure Compromise
- R009 Sensitive Information Theft / Extortion
- R010 Unauthorized / Excessive Access
- R012 Malicious Insider
- R014 Lost or Stolen Device
- R018 Third-Party Technology Failure / Compromise
- R019 Infinity Contractor Dependency / Compromise
- R024 Unauthorized Physical Access
- R027 Backup / Recovery Failure
- R028 Data Corruption
- R029 Vendor Fraud
- R030 Sales Fraud
- R031 Payroll Fraud
- R032 Inventory Theft

Risk treatment is documented in [[RTP-001 Enterprise Risk Treatment Plan]].

---

# 81. Exceptions

Exceptions to this policy require:

- Documented business justification
- Affected requirement
- Associated risk
- Defined duration
- Compensating controls where practical
- Responsible owner
- Appropriate approval

Emergency incident-response action does not require advance exception approval when delay would materially increase harm.

Such actions remain subject to subsequent review.

---

# 82. Enforcement

Failure to comply with this policy may result in:

- Access restriction
- Corrective action
- Management review
- Personnel action
- Contractual action
- Legal action where appropriate

Intentional concealment of a significant security incident must be escalated.

---

# 83. Policy Review

This policy must be reviewed:

- At least annually
- Following a material security incident
- Following significant incident-response exercises
- Following significant assessment findings
- When incident-response responsibilities materially change
- When significant technology or business changes affect response capability

The CIO coordinates review.

Material revisions require appropriate approval.

---

# 84. Traceability

Incident response must support the enterprise security lifecycle:

**Security Event → Analysis → Incident → Containment → Investigation → Evidence → Recovery → Finding → Corrective Action → Validation → Risk Reassessment → Control Improvement**

Incident response also participates in the broader enterprise traceability model maintained in [[REG-007 Cross-Reference Matrix]].
