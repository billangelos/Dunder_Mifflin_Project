# Student Project Summary

## Learning GRC by Building an Enterprise Security Program

This project began while I was studying for the Certified Information Systems Security Professional (CISSP) certification.

As I worked through the CISSP material, I found myself learning about Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), risk management, security policies, Business Impact Analysis (BIA), Business Continuity/Disaster Recovery (BC/DR), Identity and Access Management (IAM), security operations, incident response, security assessment, and many other subjects.

I could understand many of these concepts individually, but I wanted a way to connect them.

Reading about risk assessments, policies, security controls, incident response, business continuity, auditing, and governance is useful. Studying these subjects individually, however, doesn't necessarily show how they fit together inside an organization.

I wanted to understand the connections.

I didn't want to just memorize definitions for the CISSP exam. I wanted to understand questions such as:

- Why does a company need a particular security control?
- Where does that requirement come from?
- What business process is the control protecting?
- What risk is being reduced?
- How do you know whether the control actually exists?
- How do you know whether it works?
- What evidence would an assessor expect to see?
- What happens when a control fails?
- How does a technical problem eventually become something management or executives need to understand?
- How do Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery requirements relate to business needs?
- How do policies, standards, procedures, controls, evidence, testing, findings, remediation, and risk all connect?

Those questions became the foundation of this project.

What started as a way to reinforce my CISSP studies gradually became something much larger. Instead of studying GRC concepts independently, I decided to build a fictional company environment where I could apply them and see how they interacted.

The completed project represents more than **100 hours of work over approximately six weeks**.

That time wasn't spent asking an AI system to generate a finished security program. It was spent building the company, discussing assumptions, learning concepts, making decisions, developing artifacts, challenging recommendations, correcting mistakes, following dependencies, reviewing the results, and gradually building my understanding of how an enterprise GRC program fits together.

# Proposed Controls Are Not Existing Controls

Early in the project, it would have been easy to identify a weakness, propose a control, and immediately treat the risk as if the problem had been solved.

That isn't how risk management works.

A written policy doesn't prove that a control exists.

An approved project doesn't prove that a technology has been deployed.

A deployed technology doesn't prove that it has been configured correctly.

A configured control doesn't prove that it operates consistently.

A control that operated successfully once doesn't necessarily prove that it will continue operating effectively.

Because of this, the project adopted an important rule:

> **Proposed controls receive no credit against current residual risk until they are implemented and appropriately validated.**

This rule changed the way I looked at risk.

The project distinguishes among concepts such as:

- Inherent risk
- Existing controls
- Current residual risk
- Proposed treatments
- Target residual risk
- Implemented controls
- Validated controls
- Post-treatment risk

Target residual risk is a prediction.

It represents where we believe risk may be after treatment.

It isn't proof that the organization has reached that level of risk.

# Learning Through Dependency

Another major lesson was that GRC work has an order.

Many security decisions depend on information that must be established earlier.

For example, I learned why it would be incorrect to just choose Recovery Time Objectives (RTOs) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPOs) because they sounded reasonable.

The business must first be understood.

The sequence developed in the project was:

**Business Requirement → Criticality → Maximum Tolerable Downtime → RTO / RPO → Recovery Strategy → Technical Design → Implementation → Testing**

This same dependency appeared throughout the project.

The larger project developed approximately as:

**Business → Assets → Information → Business Impact → Risk → Governance → Architecture → Controls → Procedures → Operations → Evidence → Assessment → Findings → Remediation → Risk Reassessment**

Once I began seeing those relationships, GRC stopped looking like a collection of unrelated documents.

It started looking like a system.

---

# The Traceability Model

Eventually, the project developed a traceability model that became one of the central ideas of the entire repository:

**Business Requirement → Asset / Process → Classification / BIA → Risk → Treatment → Policy / Standard → Control → Procedure → Implementation → Evidence → Test / Audit → Finding → Remediation → Retest → Risk Reassessment**

This helped answer a question I had when I began studying GRC:

**Why do all of these documents exist?**

Each artifact has a job.

A risk assessment identifies and evaluates risk.

A treatment plan determines what the organization intends to do about that risk.

A policy establishes management requirements.

A standard translates requirements into more specific expectations.

A control is intended to produce a security outcome.

A procedure explains how an activity is performed.

Implementation establishes the capability.

Evidence helps demonstrate what actually happened.

Assessment determines whether the requirement or control is satisfied.

A finding documents a supported conclusion.

Corrective action addresses a deficiency.

Retesting determines whether the correction worked.

Risk reassessment determines what the new evidence means to the organization.

Once these relationships became clear, many GRC concepts that had previously seemed abstract became much easier for me to understand.

# Evidence Changed How I Thought About Controls

One of the biggest changes in my thinking came when the project moved from designing controls to asking how those controls would be proven.

It is easy to write:

> Privileged access must be reviewed.

It is much harder to answer:

- Who performs the review?
- How often?
- Which accounts are included?
- What evidence is produced?
- Where is that evidence retained?
- How would an assessor select a sample?
- What would constitute failure?
- What happens when an exception is discovered?
- Who owns remediation?
- How is remediation validated?
- Does the result change the risk assessment?

Those questions turned a security statement into an assessable control.

That was a major step in understanding GRC.

# Assurance

The project eventually developed an assurance model.

Assessment methods include concepts such as:

- Inspection
- Observation
- Interviews
- Sampling
- Technical testing
- Reconciliation
- Independent Performance

The purpose isn't to manufacture evidence showing that the security program works.

The purpose is to determine what is actually happening.

A successful assessment may conclude that a control is effective.

It may also conclude that a control is:

- Partially Effective
- Ineffective
- Not Implemented

That is useful information.

Finding a weakness during an assessment doesn't mean the assessment failed.

It means the assessment did its job.

---

# Reporting at Different Levels

The project also helped me understand why the same security information shouldn't be presented identically to everyone.

We eventually developed three reporting levels:

**Operational → Execution, Evidence, Exceptions, Technical Detail**

**Management → Accountability, Performance, Action**

**Executive → Decisions and Enterprise Risk**

An administrator may need to know exactly which server failed.

A manager may need to know why a required process repeatedly failed and who owns corrective action.

An executive may need to know how the issue affects enterprise risk, whether additional resources are required, and what decision needs to be made.

The underlying problem may be the same.

The information needed to make a decision is different.

# What I Learned About Using AI

This project also taught me a great deal about how I think AI should be used.

Working with AI should be collaborative.

AI is a tool, and I believe the best results come from treating it like one.

It can help guide you down a path toward where you want to go. Sometimes it may even get you surprisingly close with minimal prompting.

But that isn't where I found the greatest value.

The results became much better when I discussed things with it.

I asked questions.

I asked why.

I corrected it.

I gave it additional context.

I told it when something didn't make sense.

I pushed back when I disagreed.

I made it explain recommendations that I didn't understand.

I changed direction when a better idea developed.

I also had to catch mistakes.

AI-generated material shouldn't automatically be treated as correct.

During the project, I had to:

- Review output
- Question assumptions
- Correct inconsistencies
- Reject inappropriate recommendations
- Maintain the fictional business reality
- Check whether proposed controls were being mistaken for existing controls
- Verify cross-references
- Reconcile conflicting information
- Require revisions when something didn't make sense

That wasn't a weakness in the process.

It was one of the things that made the process educational.

Using AI effectively still requires judgment.

The faster AI can generate material, the more important it becomes to understand what the material actually says.

I came away from this project believing that AI can be an incredible learning tool when you actively participate in the process.

Use it to guide you.

Use it to challenge you.

Ask it questions.

Correct it when it gets something wrong.

Give it context that it doesn't have.

Push back from time to time.

Make it defend a recommendation.

Make yourself defend your own recommendation.

When you work with AI that way, rather than just asking it to produce something for you, the end results can sometimes be pretty amazing.

20. How corrective action would be tracked.
21. How the control would be retested.
22. How new evidence could eventually change residual risk.

Then ask your AI assistant to quiz you on the chain.

Don't let it give you the answers immediately.

Make yourself explain why each connection exists.

# Reverse the Traceability

Once you can follow a risk forward, go backward.

Choose a control and ask:

> Why does this control exist?

Trace it backward:

**Control → Standard / Policy → Treatment → Risk → Asset / Process → Business Requirement**

Then move forward again:

**Control → Procedure → Implementation → Evidence → Assessment → Finding → Remediation → Retest → Risk Reassessment**

Ask your AI assistant to remove one link from the chain and make you identify what is missing.

Ask it to give you a control and make you identify the risk it addresses.

Ask it to give you a finding and make you trace it back to the requirement that was violated.

This turns the repository into an interactive GRC lab.

6. How serious is it?

8. When should it be completed?

# Practice Reporting the Same Problem Three Ways

Choose one significant security problem from the repository.

Explain it first to an administrator.

Then explain it to a manager.

Then explain it to the CEO.

Use the project's reporting principle:

**Operational → Execution, Evidence, Exceptions, Technical Detail**

**Management → Accountability, Performance, Action**

**Executive → Decisions and Enterprise Risk**

Ask your AI assistant to play each audience.

Have it respond as the administrator, manager, or CEO.

Let it ask you questions.

If the fictional CEO starts asking technical questions that an executive shouldn't need to ask, consider whether your report failed to explain the business risk.

This is a practical way to learn that good GRC communication depends on the audience.

# Final Advice to Another GRC Student

Don't judge your progress by how many security documents you can produce.

Ask yourself whether you understand why they exist.

Don't accept a risk score because someone else calculated it.

Try to defend your own.

Don't accept a control because it sounds secure.

Ask what risk it addresses.

Don't accept implementation as proof of effectiveness.

Ask for evidence.

Don't treat a finding as a failure of the assessment process.

A well-supported finding may mean the assessment process worked exactly as intended.

Don't be afraid to disagree with this project.

You aren't supposed to reproduce my answers.

Use the company I built as your foundation and come up with your own.

If you can explain why you would make a different decision, identify the consequences, and support your position with evidence and sound risk reasoning, you're learning from it correctly.

Use AI, but don't hand your learning over to it.

Work with it.

Question it.

Correct it.

Challenge it.

Let it challenge you.

Use it as the tool it is.

Most importantly, keep following the connections.

**Business → Assets → Impact → Risk → Governance → Controls → Operations → Evidence → Assurance → Improvement**

That is where this project became useful to me, and that is where I believe it can become useful to someone else.
